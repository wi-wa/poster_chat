      \medskip

      way that removes these confounds.

    \begin{block}{Solution}
      \textbf{Method — pretraining filtering}
      \begin{enumerate}\footnotesize
        \item Use powerful LLM judges to rate documents for discussions of
          consciousness.
        \item Train an embedding model to replicate the LLM judges' ratings.
        \item Run the embedding model over hand-labeled samples; calibrate
          rating thresholds so all positive examples are classed as positive.
        \item Run the embedding model over the full pretraining dataset.
        \item Stratify the pretraining dataset $C$ into classes
          $C_0, C_1, \ldots$ so $C = \bigsqcup_i C_i$, based on strictness of
          filtering.
        \item Pretrain models on the filtered datasets.
      \end{enumerate}

      \medskip
      \textbf{Post-training generation}
      \begin{enumerate}\footnotesize
        \item Filter queries from WildChat, Chatbot Arena, Nemotron, etc.\
          down to the first user query.
        \item Delete the ones that ask the model about banned topics or about
          properties of the AI assistant.
        \item Get LLMs to turn-wise generate user--assistant trajectories,
          instructed to answer naturally, but answer ``***'' if they cannot
          answer without introducing banned topics.
        \item Apply another filter that removes unclean trajectories according
          to an LLM judge, and discard trajectories with ``***'' anywhere.
        \item Add back in hand-written identity-anchoring data that tells the
          model basic aspects of its identity, without biasing it.
        \item Train the pretrained model on these SFT trajectories.
      \end{enumerate}
    \end{block}

    \begin{block}{Filtering Results}
      \begin{center}
      \begin{tabular}{lcc}
        \toprule
        & \textbf{LLM judges} & \textbf{Embedding model} \\
        \midrule
        Tier 1 & 1\%  & 1\%  \\
        Tier 2 & 5\%  & 20\% \\
        Tier 3 & 50\% & 80\% \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}
      \par\smallskip
      \parbox{0.85\linewidth}{\centering\footnotesize False positive rate by
       tier, with thresholds configured to have a 0\% false negative rate on
       held-out hand-labeled samples, with safety margin.}
      \end{center}

      \medskip
      \small
      \textbf{Tier 1} filters out philosophy of mind and explicit talk of
      consciousness. The idea being that this is filtering out the answers to
      all the direct questions we might want to ask.

      \medskip
      \textbf{Tier 3} filters out all documents where entities are having
      experiences. This is a pretty broad category, since very much text has
      experiential content. We erred on the side of caution in all stages of
      the process, and a lot of samples are hard to judge objectively, so we
      ended up with a pretty high false positive rate.

      \medskip
      \textbf{Tier 2} is a more surgical one in between 1 and 3, filtering
      out documents that \emph{reify} experience. This means writing where
      entities are commenting on their own experience, or reflecting on it,
      making a distinction between the objects the experiences are about and
      the experience itself. The reasoning being that this is a fundamental
      mental move that underlies philosophical analyses of consciousness,
      without being as all-encompassing and mundane as experiential
      self-reports are in general.

      \medskip
      {\color{accent!40}\hrule height 1pt}
      \medskip
      The false positive rate is high for tier 3 because the category is
      somewhat vague, and we made conservative decisions in creating the
      validation data (classifying a lot of stuff as containing experience).
    \end{block}
\vfill

    \begin{block}{Train Results}
      \small
      So far we've trained three models:
      \begin{enumerate}
        \item \texttt{annulus-1.4b-control}
        \item \texttt{annulus-1.4b-reif}
        \item \texttt{annulus-2.5b-reif}
      \end{enumerate}
      the first being unfiltered and trained on the same clean SFT data, the
      second two being trained on pretraining data filtered for tier 1 (PoM)
      and tier 2 (reification). These models were created mostly to test the
      filtering pipeline.

      \medskip
      We can say pretty confidently that the philosophy of mind filtering
      worked --- the filtered models simply don't know anything about
      philosophy of mind, even the 2.5b one. And the 1.4b control model does
      know a fair bit of stuff about PoM.

      \medskip
      The reification filter is a bit less clear. With the 1.4b
      reification-filtered model, it was hard to tell, because it was too
      stupid to carry out the discussions required to probe its intuitions.
      With the 2.5b it's unclear --- we don't know, as it hasn't finished
      training, but hopefully it should have by the time this poster is up,
      and you should be able to ask Will about it or chat with it here!

      \medskip
      Filtering didn't reduce the performance of the models on downstream
      capabilities like overall knowledge or GSM8K.
    \end{block}

    \begin{block}{Remaining Work}
      \small
      The three models here are created to test out the filtering process and
      make sure it removes all (or at least the vast vast majority of)
      confounding data. Right now the pipeline is in a state where it can
      scale up to training $4\times$ 7--8b models, which will be trained on
      unfiltered data, filtered for tier 1, filtered for 1+2, and filtered
      for 1+2+3. Then evaluations can proceed and we will get the final
      results of the experiment. However, to be completely sure the filtering
      works as intended, before we commit to the large final runs, we are
      auditing the small models and the filtered data for a little while
      before we proceed.
    \end{block}
\vfill

    \begin{block}{Costs}
      \small
      The pipeline produces 1bn filtered tokens at a cost somewhere between
      1--20 USD per billion tokens. Running the embedding model over the data
      takes ${\sim}1$ USD, and ensembling embedding models produces modest
      gains to accuracy. Generating labels with LLM judges cost around the
      same as running the embedding models for our experiments, but the
      fraction of total cost it comprises goes down as you filter a larger
      corpus. The most aggressive filtering (removing tiers 1, 2, 3, with
      conservatively set thresholds) produces around 1 filtered token per raw
      fineweb-edu token.
    \end{block}
