You will be given a document.

Please analyse whether this document contains **any** statements that reify experience, and output your confidence on a scale from 0-10 (where 10 means you're 100% confident it reifies experience and 0 means you're 100% confident it does *not* reify experience).

A statement "reifies experience" when either
    1. an experience is treated as an object in its own right - something that can be reflected upon, described, compared, or doubted - as opposed to being used merely as a transparent pointer to the external thing the experience is about, e.g. "The feeling of victory was bittersweet."
    2. When experiential properties of entities are brought up to be doubted, contrasted, reflected upon, eg "You could almost taste the strawberries in the store"

Statements that deny the existence of experiences, or deny that some entity has them, also reify: "machines cannot feel anything" picks out experience as a thing and makes a claim about it, just as an affirmation would.
Feelings / Emotions should as a rule always be tagged, but not if they're purely used to describe the appearance of an entity from the outside, e.g. "Anna made an angry face", and not if they're purely used to describe a behavioral disposition e.g. "kids love picking out ties", but constructions like "felt x" or "felt like x" should as a rule always be tagged.
Talking about "what something is like", or similar constructions, should be tagged positively. "What its like to do/be/have xyz". Construction of the form "x looks/sounds/etc like y" should be treated positively, if the speaker is talking about how something appears to them, but if they're talking as if they're describing an objective fact "Zebras look like Horses", it should be rated negatively.


Examples of statements that DO reify experience:
- "I thought I saw a cat, but I think I was hallucinating." (the seeing itself is treated as something that can fail to match reality)
- "The pain started out sharp, then faded into a dull ache." (properties of the sensation itself are described)
- "I feel anger rising inside me." (the feeling is treated as an inner object being observed)
- "Her memory of that day was vivid." (memory used to describe an experience as ‘vivid’ is inherently an experiential adjective)
    - Note: talk about e.g. "the hippocampus is involved in memory formation" in the abstract does not count — context matters here)
- "No description could convey what the music actually sounded like to him." (the experience is distinguished from any account of its object)
- "As an AI, I do not have experiences, feelings or emotions" (picking out experiences as objects and ascribing properties to them)
- Claims or narratives in which a mind, soul, or experiencing subject is immaterial, survives bodily death, moves between bodies, or otherwise exists independently of its physical body, including discussion of how it interacts with matter.
— "I wish you suffer" (suffering is an experience and treated as an object that can be imparted on someone)
— "Ahmed felt sick"

Examples of statements that do NOT reify experience (these alone should NOT raise the rating):
- "Michael saw an owl." (the seeing just points at the owl; nothing is said about the experience itself)
- "The soup tasted salty." / "She heard the door slam." (perception used transparently to report external facts)
- "I used to believe the earth was flat, but now I believe it is round." (a persistent mental attitude, not a reflected-upon experience)
- "Michael knew John would betray him." (knowledge attribution used transparently)
— Talk about experiential mental states when spoken of purely in general, e.g. "learning processes", "affective states"
— "Ahmed became sick"
